# Accounts and Subscriptions to Close

*Recurring charges don't know that anything happened. They keep billing the
way alarm clocks ring — on schedule, no matter what. This chapter is the
hunt: a calm, systematic way to find every recurring charge, sort them into
a safe canceling order, and stop them without breaking anything the
household still needs. One real warning for the feelings side: you will find
charges that carry their own little wave of grief (the guitar-lesson app he
used every Sunday). That's normal. Take the break; the list keeps.*

---

## Step 1: gather the full billing picture

You can't cancel what you can't find, and families routinely discover
subscriptions for two or three years. Gather from all of these sources —
each finds what the others miss:

- **The wallet and saved cards.** Every autopay you can remember, plus the
  docs in the wallet: gym tags, transit passes, warehouse-store cards.
- **Bank and credit card statements, 3–6 months back** (longer catches the
  annual renewals). Highlight every recurring line. Different amounts each
  month still count — utilities count too, they're just at step 3's safe
  list.
- **Search the person's email** (if you have access under whatever permission
  you legally have — the institution will confirm that, not you): the
  keywords below, one at a time. Screenshots or print any hit before acting,
  because support lines will ask "how did you get access to this account?"
  (truthful answer: "as family handling arrangements — what do you need
  from me to proceed?")
  - `receipt` · `invoice` · `subscription` · `renewal` · `auto-renew`
  - `billing` · `payment received` · `welcome to` · `thanks for joining`
  - `your order` · `account created` · `password reset` · `verification code`
- **On their phone(s):** the app-store subscription screens (Apple's
  "Subscriptions" and Google Play's "Payments & subscriptions") reveal
  years of app memberships in one list.
- **The mail sweep:** every forwarded envelope with a company you don't
  recognize is a row in the tracker. ([Mail chapter explains the
  forwarding first step.](05-mail-and-benefits.md))
- **Tax software, streaming, and cloud accounts** often surface on bank
  statements under unfamiliar publisher names — search the amount online
  plus the word "charge" and it names itself quickly.

## Step 2: sort into three boxes

Not everything should be canceled, and canceling in the wrong order can
break the household. Sort every finding into:

- **Box A — The keep-running set.** Mortgage/rent, electric, gas, water,
  trash, internet, phone for a home people still live in, car insurance for
  cars that are driven or parked legally, health insurance for survivors,
  pharmacy needs. *These keep running until formally handled with their own
  institution — a lapse here can mean a cold house or a coverage gap, so
  handle them "with," not "against."*
- **Box B — Subscriptions.** Streaming, gyms, meal kits, apps, magazines,
  cloud storage, clubs, credit-monitoring, antivirus renewals, delivery
  memberships. Canceling these early won't break the household — it's Box A
  that carries that risk. They're just not all emotionally the same size.
- **Box C — Debts and one-time charges.** Credit cards, personal loans, final
  medical bills. These mostly get handled through the estate process and
  patient mail-watching rather than friendly cancellations — see
  [the don't-pay-yet principle](05-mail-and-benefits.md#the-dont-pay-yet-principle) and
  ask each creditor "what's your process when an account holder has died?"

## Step 3: the safe order

1. **Box A with the household's realities:** transfer or update in the name
   of whoever keeps living there. Call the *current* insurer for a parked
   car: "storage-only" coverage options exist — the agent asks the
   situation, nobody assumes.
2. **Box B smallest-to-biggest emotional load:** start with two easy wins
   (a streaming service, a magazine). The first cancellation teaches the
   muscle; the fourth is just admin. Save cloud storage, email, and photo
   accounts for last — anything holding years of memories gets handled
   *after* its contents are secured. (The digital-legacy-handbook's
   close-out checklist is built exactly for that step; it's a companion
   project, deliberately not repeated here.)
3. **Box C by mail and patience**, as the estate professional or agencies
   direct, always in writing.

## The subscription call, in two scripts

> **The standard cancel.** "Hi, my name is ____________. I'm calling about
> an account for ____________, who passed away on ____________. I need to
> close the membership and stop all billing. What do you need from me, and
> will you send written confirmation of the closure?"

> **The surprise-charge version.** For charges you didn't know about: "I
> found these charges on a bank statement while handling my family member's
> passing. I'd like to understand this account, stop future billing, and
> ask whether unused time can be refunded — what's your process?"

Three habits on every one of these calls:

- Ask the cancellation question directly: *"Is there anything besides this
  one that will keep billing us separately?"* (family plans routinely split
  into their own surprise charges).
- Get the **confirmation in writing** — email or letter with a reference
  number. A phone promise is a memory; paper is a record that goes in the
  [binder's done tab](09-binder-checklist.md).
- Screenshot anything canceled in an app or on a website, and log the date
  + screenshot's name in the tracker. If a charge later reappears, the
  screenshot plus the reference number usually settles it with one call.

## Common categories and their traps

| Category | What to know before you call |
| --- | --- |
| Streaming services | Usually self-cancel online or in the app; look for **"cancel,"** not "pause" (pause keeps billing later). Shared or family plans: ask whether the plan itself or only member seats should change. |
| Gyms and fitness | These often want **written notice** or an in-person stop; some contracts have steep end-fees. Ask for their bereavement/death-of-member policy by name — many have one. Always ask for a cancellation number. |
| App-store subscriptions (Apple/Google) | Cancel on the store's subscription screen, not the app's own settings (the app hides the truth). Ask support whether unused paid time after a death can be refunded — some can. |
| Meal kits and scheduled deliveries | Skip the next box first (it buys you time), then cancel; ask about any already-paid boxes on the way. |
| Cloud storage, email, photo backups | Do this **last**, and only after backing up what matters (photos, letters, saved files). Years of family history sit in exactly these accounts. |
| Internet, TV, landline | Ask about equipment return rules and final-bill timing. For **landlines**: ask about keeping the number briefly if you're not sure yet — 2-factor texts and old contacts surface for months. |
| Cell phone lines | Multiple lines on one plan are common: ask to drop a line without dropping a plan a household needs; ask the store's return rules for their device. |
| Electric / gas / water | **Live-in survivors:** transfer service to your name; ask for the final read on the old account and any deposit refund. Lapse warnings are real here — these are Box A on purpose. |
| Insurance policies | Car/home may be canceled by the estate; **life insurance is a claim, not a cancellation** — forms start with the insurer or employer. Health insurance usually changes at the employer or Medicare level. Three different processes; don't let anyone bundle them by mistake. |
| Clubs, fraternal orgs, churches, veterans' posts | Some of these include real death benefits nobody used — ask plainly, "does this organization have a death benefit we should know about?" Also ask about refunding unused dues. |
| Professional licenses, unions | Ask about prorated refunds and who to notify on record; usually postal mail with a certificate copy works. |
| Credit monitoring and identity services | Once the three bureaus have the file flagged deceased (see [the bureaus](05-mail-and-benefits.md#the-credit-bureaus)), a monitoring service's job mostly ends; cancel the renewal, keep the credit freeze. |
| Warehouse stores & retail memberships | Prorated refunds are sometimes available to family members; ask — it's one question. |
| Websites, domains, hosting | Renewal dates happen yearly; transferring or closing lives in the digital-legacy-handbook's close-out checklists (by design, not duplicated here). |
| Tax prep software, antivirus, utilities' "protection plans" | These renew quietly; find them on statements and in in-box "receipt" searches above. Cancel the renewal; keep any current-year records. |

## The tracker

Put every finding in one row: company, kind, amount, where you found it,
how to cancel, status, notes. Print it or keep it as a file — it's the
worksheet version of this chapter, and by the end it doubles as the
"what did we actually pay for all those years" story.

The fillable file is **[worksheets/subscriptions-tracker.csv](../worksheets/subscriptions-tracker.csv)**
— open it in any spreadsheet app on your own machine; no account needed.
