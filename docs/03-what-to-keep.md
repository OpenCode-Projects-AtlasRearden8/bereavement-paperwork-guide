# What to Keep Copies of, For Good

*You can't know today which piece of paper is going to fix a problem five
years from now — so the quiet, boring rule that wins is: copy everything
now, throw almost nothing away yet, and let a few categories graduate to the
"forever file." Unlike most of this guide, this chapter has almost no
deadlines. Copies are cheap now and expensive in three years.*

---

## The forever file, and why it's smaller than the shoebox

Most of what arrives in your mail can actually be released after a while.
A handful of document types are *impossible or expensive to recreate* —
and at least one of them (proof that a bill was paid, or an account
closed) resolves arguments years later. Those are:

1. **Certified death certificates** — your lifetime supply. Most agencies
   that ask for one keep a copy; the stock you ordered (5–10+ is the common
   suggestion) is what gets you through the year. Losing count of how many
   you ordered is fine; the funeral home's receptionist remembers.
2. **Everything on this page's checklist below** — each line explains itself.

Two helpful distinctions before the list:

- **Originals back, copies kept.** Many agencies want to *see* an original,
  photocopied it, and mailed it back. When you mail an original, note the
  date and destination in the [call log](08-call-log.md) and expect its
  return — then keep that agency's own "proof" letter, which is cheaper to
  use than a certificate.
- **Copies made by the institution outrank your copies.** A closure letter
  from the bank (with letterhead and a reference number) settles questions
  your photocopy of the statement can't. Ask for paper on every closing
  call: *"Can you mail or email written confirmation that the account is
  closed?"*

## The forever file checklist

Keep these **permanently** (two places — see below):

- [ ] Certified death certificates (receipt of how many you ordered + the
      unspend ones, counted)
- [ ] Will or trust documents plus any lawyer correspondence, and probate
      court filings (date-stamped)
- [ ] **Letters testamentary / of administration** (the court paper that
      says you're officially the one who can act) — [plain English in the
      glossary](10-glossary.md)
- [ ] Every "proof of death" or "confirmation of notice" letter an agency
      sends (Social Security, [the benefits letters](05-mail-and-benefits.md),
      insurance acknowledgment letters, the pension plan's letters)
- [ ] Life insurance: claim forms' reference numbers, the policy's
      beneficiary confirmation, the payout confirmation letter, and the
      check's copy or payment record
- [ ] Prepaid funeral plan / pre-need contract and its final settlement
- [ ] Final tax return(s) for your person, plus IRS/state acceptance,
      any estate income-tax returns, and refund confirmations
- [ ] Account closure letters and **zero-balance statements** — every bank,
      card, loan, and subscription you closed
- [ ] Paid-in-full and lien-release letters for car loans and the mortgage;
      the re-titled/auto-released title and the recorded deed afterward
- [ ] Vehicle title transfer papers and registration confirmations
- [ ] Receipts for anything *you* paid *from your own pocket* for the estate
      (funeral costs, storage unit, travel, court filing fees) — these can
      be reimbursable from the estate, and even when they're not, they're
      the record of what you carried
- [ ] Medical: the **final, adjusted** final bill and the insurance
      explanation-of-benefits (EOB) pages for the last months — medical
      billing corrections arrive for months; keep every "this replaces your
      earlier bill" letter
- [ ] The [call log](08-call-log.md) — completed, signed by nothing, just
      kept. It's the narrative of the whole process.

## How long to keep the rest

| Keep for | What usually lives here | Why this long |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Forever | Everything above | These are the papers that can't be regenerated or settle arguments |
| 7 years | Bank and card statements at closure, tax papers' supporting documents, final medical billing, household insurance claim papers | Common practice aligned with how long tax and dispute windows stay open (this is general education, not a rule for your exact situation) |
| 1–2 years | Utility closure confirmations, subscription cancellation letters, housing move-out records, deposit refunds | Issues usually surface within a billing cycle or two; a year of keep catches them all |
| Days–weeks | Catalogs, condolence-card envelopes' extras, promotional mail | Photograph anything that matters (a card front, a kind note) and let the rest go |

*The 7-year and 1–2-year rows are common practice ranges, stated plainly as
education — not a legal standard. When in doubt, the cost of storing a
folder in a box for another year is famously low.*

## The two-places rule

One copy of everything important lives in the
[binder](09-binder-checklist.md). The second copy — scans and photos —

- lives on **two separate drives or devices** you control (a laptop plus a
  USB drive in a different building, or in a bank box is a fine second
  place), *encrypted or at least password-protected*,
- is named so future-you can find things: `2026-10-27_death-certificate_1.pdf`
  style (date, then what it is) beats "scan123.pdf" every single time,
- and never, ever goes anywhere public — no email attachments to check with
  strangers, no shared photo albums with "estate" in the folder name.

Scanning tip: the phone in your pocket is a scanner. Photograph each page on
a dark table with the flash off and the page flat; apps you already own do
the rest. Then the paper originals get their second place: a fire box, a
safe, or a bank safety-deposit box (call the bank first to ask their rules,
because access rules for a decedent's box are one of the real reasons to
make that call early).

## Where the copies live, physically

The [binder checklist](09-binder-checklist.md) has the tabs; the short
version is: copies in the binder in their topic's tab, originals in the
fire box or safe or bank box, and the *scan* in two digital places. Three
seams, two rules: nothing valuable lives in single copies, and nothing
important lives only "somewhere in the house."

## Retiring paper, eventually

When a document's keep-for window (table above) has passed:

- **Shred, never recycle whole documents.** A cross-cut shredder ($ used
  ones exist at every thrift store) or a community shredding day; hospitals,
  banks, and some pharmacies host free shred events.
- **Cross-cut rule:** anything with a signature, an account number, or a
  Social Security number gets shredded, not binned.
- **The forever file itself never leaves your control.** If you're ready for
  a next-keeper-of-papers conversation years from now, that's a handoff (one
  person, in person, one afternoon — and a signed note of what changed
  hands). *And if you're preparing all of this for your own someday, that's
  the digital-legacy-handbook's territory — its when-I'm-gone folder and
  close-out checklists are the "before" version of this chapter.*
