# Mail and Benefits Basics

*The mail does two opposite things after a death: it keeps delivering
problems (bills, renewals, collection letters), and it's also the system
that eventually surfaces everything the family missed — benefits, refunds,
final statements you'd never have found another way. Set up the forwarding,
then watch the mail on a schedule, and it turns from a trickle of anxiety
into a working search engine.*

---

## Handling their mail

- **Set up forwarding first.** Ask at the post office counter about
  forwarding mail for someone who has died — it's a specific process, and
  the clerk will tell you which proof they need (a certificate copy and ID
  are the usual ideas; let the counter confirm the current rule). Forwarding
  catches names you don't know about yet, which is the entire point.
- **Watch the mail on a schedule, not by dread.** One weekly sweep for the
  first six months, then monthly for a year after that, catches essentially
  everything: late-to-arrive bills, insurance IDs, benefit letters, records
  requests. Every sweep is two minutes or five; the calendar entry is the
  whole trick.
- **Open everything that has their name on it** — even official-looking
  letters you'd rather not, and even things addressed to "the estate of."
  An envelope isn't bad news; it's a *name for the call log* ninety percent
  of the time. Stack an "open this week" pile and a "someday/hard" pile,
  and [pace the hard pile](07-self-pacing.md) without guilt.
- **Checks that arrive made out to your person after death:** don't deposit
  them into your own account, ever. Every institution treats this as a
  formal thing — the check belongs to the estate, and the bank or agency
  that sent it will tell you their mechanics. Ask, then follow.
- **Keep a "letters arrived" line in the [call log](08-call-log.md)** with
  the date; when a bill turns out to have been sent twice, or a letter
  "never arrived" back at the bureau, the log is the answer.

## Government benefits worth checking

(U.S. examples; every country runs the same shape with its own names.)

- **Social Security** (`www.ssa.gov` — one of the few federal agencies with
  a short, famously polite call flow): news out of this call changes the
  family's finances — [survivor benefits](#survivor-benefits), the one-time
  lump-sum death payment for eligible widows/widowers/children, and clarity
  about payments that arrived around the date of death. Call within days
  if you can; ask one plain question and let them steer: *"Is anyone in
  this family eligible for anything, and does anything need to be
  returned?"*
- **Medicare**: coverage through Medicare itself generally ends with the
  person; notifying Social Security handles the record-keeping. If a
  Medicare *Advantage* or drug plan was in play, the plan's card has its
  own number — call it to ask how coverage ended and whether anything
  (like a final premium) is owed.
- **Medicaid and state programs:** if your person received Medicaid, SNAP,
  state disability, or senior programs, each state office has its own
  reporting step. Ask each one plainly; these calls are short and staffed
  for it.
- **Veterans' affairs (VA):** if they served, survivors may be eligible for
  benefits — pensions stop through one process, survivor benefits and
  burial allowances start through another, as do headstone, flag, and
  burial-in-a-national-cemetery questions. The number to reach VA benefits
  is at `www.va.gov`; the funeral home may also file some VA paperwork for
  you — ask.
- **The IRS** (`www.irs.gov`): a final income tax return may be due for the
  year of death, and a refund might be coming; the IRS publishes plain
  guidance for survivors (search for **IRS Publication 559**). When in
  doubt about anything tax-shaped, one hour with a tax pro is the wise
  purchase — this guide is education, not tax advice.
- **Unclaimed property:** every state maintains a free official search
  (search *"your state's name"* + **unclaimed property** — .gov results).
  Old deposits, dormant accounts, and forgotten checks surface there, and
  claims are free; anyone who wants a fee for this visit is on the
  [red-flag list](06-predatory-help-red-flags.md).

## Survivor benefits

The longest word in this guide, the simplest reality behind it: several
systems pay *surviving family members*, but nearly all of them pay **only
after someone applies**. Nothing automatic lands in a bank account. The
windows range from years down to a few months depending on the program, so
 sooner is safer than perfect:

- **Social Security survivor benefits:** widows/widowers (including in many
  cases divorced spouses), children, and dependent parents each have their
  own rules. Don't try to learn the rules from a page — say: *"What's
  available to us, and what would you need to start it?"* Take notes.
- **The lump-sum death payment** ($255 as of recent years, to an eligible
  surviving spouse or child) is a real thing with a real application.
- **Benefit payments that arrive after death:** if a payment for your
  person arrives after they died, ask the paying agency how they want it
  handled — benefits for the month of a death commonly have to be
  returned. It's a normal, frequent situation; they will not judge.
- **Employer and retirement plans:** survivor elections on pensions, 401(k)s
  and similar accounts follow **beneficiary forms**, which name a person
  directly and usually bypass the will entirely — that surprises plenty of
  families every year. HR and the plan's number on the statement will walk
  through what's on file.

## The credit bureaus

Three small calls that prevent a large, ugly strain of identity theft
(vile, and common):

1. Get on record with each of the **three credit bureaus** (Equifax,
   Experian, TransUnion) that the person is deceased and ask for the
   deceased-flag to be placed and the file **frozen**. Their sites describe
   their current process — it usually involves mailing a certificate copy
   and following by phone; use certified mail so there's a receipt,
   [which goes in the forever file](03-what-to-keep.md).
2. Watch the mail afterward: any *new* credit activity in your person's
   name is exactly the kind of envelope a weekly sweep is designed to catch
   — and it goes straight to the [red flags chapter](06-predatory-help-red-flags.md).

## The don't-pay-yet principle

The clearest, most restful idea in this whole guide, so it gets its own
name. In the U.S., a person's debts are generally paid by their estate —
out of the estate's assets — and family members don't automatically inherit
debts by existing. That's general education, not a ruling for your family's
accounts: secured debts (a car loan, a mortgage) have real paths like
surrender or assumption, joint accounts and co-signers change the picture,
and state rules vary. So pay the household's own Box A bills
([see the sorting in the subscriptions chapter](04-accounts-and-subscriptions.md)),
and for the rest, collect facts patiently — ask every creditor "what's your
process here?" in writing, and don't let any stranger's deadline pressure
pick your payment method. [Scammers know exactly how this week feels.](06-predatory-help-red-flags.md)

One practical sequence that respects the principle: notice the bill, log it,
keep the envelope, and wait for the estate role or professional advice to
arrive. Most bills are more patient than your fear of them.
