# Predatory "Help": Red Flags

*A death makes a family briefly wonderful at being generous and briefly
terrible at being skeptical — decision fatigue does that. Scam and pressure
tactics aim at exactly this window, and modern tools write flawless letters
and leave perfect-sounding voicemails now, so polish proves nothing
anymore. This chapter is a pattern table plus a few verification habits.
It is the most protective page in this guide.*

---

## The pattern table

| What arrives or is said | The tell | What you do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An unsolicited call about an "estate matter," "unclaimed inheritance," or "debt resolution" for the deceased | You never contacted them, and real institutions don't work by cold-calling grief | "What department? I'll call you back on the number from your official site." Then hang up and verify |
| "To release the funds, just pay a fee" — by gift card, wire transfer, crypto, or a payment app | No legitimate government agency or real bank ever wants payment this way | No. This is always a scam; end the call |
| Threats: arrest, suspended Social Security number, "final notice before court" | Real agencies don't threaten by phone or text, and nobody is arrested over paperwork | Hang up; you may report it (see below) |
| Urgency plus secrecy: "don't tell other family," "that bank will just slow this down," "act before close of business" | Real matters survive verification and daylight; secrecy is the scammers' tell | Slow it down hard: "I'll respond in two weeks." Legitimate files wait |
| Caller ID showing your bank's name, weird requests after | Numbers are trivially faked ("spoofed"); the display proves nothing | Call back on a number you find yourself (statement, card, or typed .gov address) |
| An email or text with a link to "process the death claim" or "verify identity" | Links are the bait; typing the site yourself is always the safe route | Never click; open a browser, type the institution's address yourself |
| Surprise "estate services" firms, unfamiliar invoices, letter-shaped demands | Real firms rarely appear from nowhere mid-grief, and real invoices arrive alongside statements you already know | Check everything against what you know; call the institution named on your own number; a real firm has a registration you can find (state bar, state records) |
| Inheritance-advance / "probate loan" offers pushed hard | Real products exist, but they are expensive and shouldn't ever feel like the only option | Never decide under pressure; compare; sleep on anything that mentions your signature |
| Door-to-door visitors (repair, moving, security, "we buy jewelry") shortly after the funeral | Obituaries — especially ones listing service *times* — are read by burglars too | Keep service times out of the public version; don't open for uninvited visitors; verify any company before inviting |
| Fake memorial fundraisers or "gofundme"-shape pages circulating | The page exists; the family didn't announce it | Give only through channels the family itself announced; when unsure, ask the family |
| "Unclaimed property" finders who want a cut | Every state runs a free official search; fees and urgency are private-priced noise | Search your own state's official unclaimed property office yourself, free |
| Pressure at the counter — funeral, cemetery, monument, estate-sale firms — to "just add this" or sign today | Grief makes every "premium" sound like the right answer; reputable providers pace with you | "We'll take the standard package today and call you about extras." Take every contract home to read. This is a normal sentence there |
| Notices that a payment "failed," demanding immediate resubmission of card details | Banks don't re-collect card numbers by call, text, or email | Contact the institution on the number on your own statement |

## Verify like a pro

Seven habits that cover essentially every pattern above:

1. **Hang up; call back on a number you found yourself.** Type the
   institution's web address yourself (or use the number printed on a
   statement you already trust). Never return a call to a number the caller
   gave you, and never trust a number from a search-result *advertisement* —
   plain results only.
2. **Collect the three facts** on every inbound call too: name, department,
   reference number. Say "I keep notes for the estate file." Scam callers
   dislike paperwork even more than you do; they usually leave.
3. **The payment-method tell.** Gift cards, crypto, wire transfers, payment
   apps, "courier collects cash," or "keep this between us" — any one of
   these means stop. Legitimate places accept checks, ordinary payments,
   patience, and written confirmations.
4. **Slow the clock on purpose.** "I'll get back to you in two weeks" is a
   complete sentence. Every deal that's real survives it.
5. **Nothing on the spot.** No signature today on anything — insurance,
   funeral extras, loans, "estate services." Take papers home. Real
   professionals hear this weekly and don't flinch.
6. **Second opinion before signing.** A bank officer, the agency's own
   representative, a local attorney (many do short, cheap consults), or one
   unexcited friend you trust. Verify with someone who has no stake.
7. **Fluency isn't proof.** AI-written scam letters are now grammatically
   perfect and warm-sounding; the old advice "look for typos" is gone. The
   new test: does the phone number, web address, and request in it check out
   when *you* type it in yourself?

## If something already went wrong

It happens to sharp people in hard weeks; the fix is speed, not shame:

1. **Money already sent:** call your bank or card issuer *immediately* —
   wire and payment-app transfers are hard to reverse, so the first hour
   matters most. Ask directly: "I was defrauded; what are my options?"
2. **Personal information shared** (Social Security number, ID copies,
   account numbers): put the credit-freeze and deceased-flag steps to work —
   see [the bureaus](05-mail-and-benefits.md#the-credit-bureaus) — and use
   the U.S. government's free recovery site **`identitytheft.gov`** for a
   step-by-step plan.
3. **A device clicked a bad link:** full antivirus scan, change that email's
   password from a *different, clean* device, and watch for follow-up
   "we saved you" calls (classic second-wave scam).
4. **Report it** so the next family gets warned: the U.S. FTC takes reports
   at **`reportfraud.ftc.gov`**; your state attorney general's site shows
   the local route. If you only block and move on, that's still a fine day's
   work.

And, because it needs saying in a chapter like this: falling for a scam is
engineered, not stupid. The one thing it should never be allowed to do is
make anyone ashamed enough to hide it from the family — hiding is the scammers'
favorite feature.
