# Self-Pacing for Shock-Brain

*A straightforward admission to start: this page exists because the rest of
this guide assumes a brain you may not currently have. Grief runs the
executive-function machinery at a fraction of its usual speed — working
memory shrinks, decisions multiply, and everything administrative suddenly
feels four sizes too big. That is not a character flaw; it's a normal,
well-documented human response to loss. What follows is the gentle structure
that gets paperwork done anyway, at a speed chosen by you.*

---

## Why everything takes four times as long now

A few honest mechanics, because knowing them lowers the self-blame:

- **Decision fatigue compounds.** A normal morning holds maybe a hundred
  small decisions; a grief morning holds those *plus* the estate's. Every
  call depletes the same battery that used to run the whole day.
- **Working memory is unreliable in the first weeks.** You will lose
  paperwork you were "just holding," forget you already called someone, and
  misremember dates. This is exactly what the [call log](08-call-log.md) is
  for — written pages are the external memory of a shock-brained person, and
  using one is not overkill, it's the professional version of normal.
- **Waves, not slopes.** Progress is not a line. A good Tuesday does not
  obligate a good Wednesday. If you treat the first six weeks as tidal, the
  low days stop feeling like personal failures.

## The minimum viable task

Behind every scary item on the big checklist, there's a ten-minute version:

- "Deal with the mail" → **open one envelope.** Log it. Done. (Yes, that was
  the whole task today, and it counts.)
- "Call Social Security" → **write the opener script on a card** and set the
  timer for tomorrow's call.
- "Organize the papers" → **make two piles** (open-this-week / hard box) and
  put the hard box somewhere with a lid.
- "Handle the estate" → **read one page of this guide.** You are, possibly
  literally, doing that right now.

The rule is: when the real task is too big, do the *announced* task — the
ten-minute version above it. Announced tasks keep the pattern alive:
something moved, something got logged, the day has a spine.

## The 15-minute timer method

Set a timer for 15 minutes and start the smallest item on today's list.
When it rings, you have a choice — stop with a clean conscience, or keep
going *only if it feels good*. Most days, one timer per day is the entire
expectation. On a good day, the timer is how you found out you had more in
you; on a bad day, the timer is why today still counted.

Pair it with the **one-call cap**: one phone call per day is a full day's
admin in the early weeks. If the day turns out to have two in it, that's a
bonus day, not the new standard.

## The phone-call sandwich

Calls are the heaviest part of paperwork, so each one gets a support
structure instead of willpower:

1. **Before (3 minutes):** the opener script from
   [who to call](02-who-to-call.md), copied onto paper or a card, plus today's
   have-ready facts. Reading a script out loud to nobody beforehand is a
   genuinely excellent technique — the words go from foggy to muscle.
2. **During:** the script covers you; you only have to hold the paper,
   not the facts.
3. **After (2 minutes):** the log entries — name, reference number, next
   step — and then one small deliberate treat (tea, a walk to the mailbox,
   the good chair for ten minutes). The treat is part of the task, not a
   reward you have to earn by finishing more.

Nobody has to do calls alone. **Book people in:** a friend on speakerphone
for the call itself, a relative who "likes forms" doing the subscription
hunt from the [tracker](04-accounts-and-subscriptions.md), an adult kid
handling the email sweep with you. This is what help is actually for —
not speeches about how you're doing, just someone sitting with you while
you make one phone call. There's even a name for that (body doubling); it
works in grief the same way it does everywhere else.

## Low-power day menu

Days serious energy can't be found. Each item takes under ten minutes:

- Open one envelope and [log it](08-call-log.md).
- Photograph one document into the scans folder (name it
  `date_what-it-is.pdf` — see [forever file](03-what-to-keep.md)).
- Add one row to the [subscriptions tracker](04-accounts-and-subscriptions.md).
- Print tomorrow's [call sheet](08-call-log.md) or write a script on paper.
- Put three loose papers into their [binder tabs](09-binder-checklist.md).
- Or the smallest thing of all: read one page of this guide. It's the task
  you may be doing right now, and it counts.

Pick *one*. A low-power day with one small switched-on item beats a
zero-item day for the confidence spiral; that's the whole reason the menu
exists.

## The hard box

Some items in the paperwork are also grief-objects: the handwritten notes,
the birthday-card stash addressed to people who won't send them now, the
personal letters, the photographs hiding in a shoebox of receipts. Sorting
through those in week two, at 40% brain, is a task traditionally assigned to
people who were never the family archivist.

So:

- Get a **box with a lid**. Grief-adjacent papers go in it unsorted, with a
  dated note on the lid ("opened November 12: inside — desk drawer, shoebox,
  filing cabinet top").
- The box is not avoidance. **It is pacing**, the same way a hospital
  schedules recovery. A lid is what makes the rest of the paperwork
  bearable paper rather than a minefield.
- Set a future date (a month or a season out) and put the date in your
  calendar as an appointment with yourself — and if that date arrives on a
  bad day, it reschedules like any appointment.

## What "done" looks like this month

Not: everything closed, every account frozen, every form filed.

Done looks like: the first-week list checked ([or as much of it as the
hospice/professionals didn't already handle](01-first-week.md)), Social
Security called, the mail forwarding started, every call logged, and the
forever file started its two-places habit. Nothing has a deadline inside
this guide's pages except where the text says so — and that patience is
deliberate design, not slackness.

If it helps, give each week a **theme** instead of a checklist: "the phone
week," "the mail week," "the subscriptions week." Themes forgive the
low-power days automatically, because a day inside the theme that did
*anything* was in theme.

## When it's bigger than pacing

This guide is admin help — it is not grief counseling, and it can't be.
There are weeks when the honest next step is a person, not a page: your
doctor, a local grief support group (hospices run excellent ones, including
for deaths that didn't involve them), a counselor, a faith community if
that's yours.

And if you, or someone near you, is genuinely not doing okay — not eating,
not sleeping, or anywhere near unsafe — that outranks every checkbox in this
guide. In the U.S., **call or text 988** anytime, day or night, for the
Suicide & Crisis Lifeline; outside the U.S., the International Association
for Suicide Prevention lists crisis lines by country at `www.iasp.info`.
Asking for that kind of help is not a paperwork failure; it's the
strongest version of the same skill this whole guide keeps practicing:
finding the right person to hand a hard thing to.

## Where you are now

You made it to the last ordinary chapter. From here, the guide becomes
tools: the [call log worksheet](08-call-log.md), the
[binder checklist](09-binder-checklist.md), and the
[glossary](10-glossary.md). Print them, or keep them on your machine.

One quiet closing thought, since pacing is the whole point: the finish line
of paperwork after a death isn't a date. It's a feeling — usually several
months in, on some weekday afternoon when you notice you did three things
and none of them hurt. The structure on this page is how you get there
without pretending anyone has to be superhuman on the way.
